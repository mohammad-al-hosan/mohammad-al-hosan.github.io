Research project

Mitigation of Short-Channel Effects in 20 nm Double-Gate MOSFETs via Step Oxide, Dual-Material Gate, and Halo Doping

A TCAD study that adds step-oxide, dual-material-gate, and halo-doping engineering to a 20 nm double-gate MOSFET one stage at a time, so the contribution and the cost of each technique can be read off separately.

Feb 2026 – Aug 2026Period
Prof. S. M. Mominuzzaman, BUETSupervisor
Silvaco Atlas TCADTool
ICECE 2026, under reviewStatus

Motivation

  • At a 20 nm channel length, drain-induced barrier lowering (DIBL), subthreshold-swing degradation and leakage all get worse. A thinner gate oxide restores gate control, but very thin oxides raise direct-tunneling leakage and reliability risk.
  • Step-oxide, dual-material-gate (DMG) and halo techniques are usually reported alone or in pairs. Here they are applied in sequence on the same planar 20 nm DG structure, so the gain from each stage, and what it costs in ION, Vth, SS and fT, is visible.
  • Goal: reduce DIBL without relying on a uniformly thin oxide.

The approach

The study starts from a plain 20 nm double-gate MOSFET and adds one fix at a time: step oxide, then a dual-material gate, then halo doping. Every stage is measured with the same metrics (DIBL, SS, ION, IOFF, with Vth and fT where relevant), so each technique's gain has a visible price, and each next stage is chosen because of what the previous one left unsolved.

Simulation setup and metrics

Silvaco Atlas, drift-diffusion transport with Van Dort quantum correction and a Gummel–Newton solver; same mesh and models for every variant; VDD = 0.8 V. Vth by constant current; DIBL from Vth at VDS = 0.08 and 0.8 V; SS over one decade below threshold; fT = gm/[2π(Cgs+Cgd)] from a 1 MHz small-signal sweep at VGS = VDS = VDD. IOFF is subthreshold channel leakage at VGS = 0. The drift-diffusion model has no tunneling component, so gate tunneling is estimated separately with a WKB expression.

Stage 1

Why not just make the oxide thinner?

The question. A thinner gate oxide gives the gate a firmer grip on the channel, so it is the obvious first fix. Does it work, and what does it cost?

What was done. Top and bottom HfO2 oxides of equal thickness (UO-DG), swept from 0.8 to 2.0 nm, with channel length, body thickness and doping fixed.

What the plots show. In the OFF state, the 0.8 nm device has the highest and flattest source-side barrier. As the oxide gets thicker, drain-induced band bending reaches further toward the source and lowers that barrier, which is why DIBL, SS and IOFF all grow.

DIBL67.21 → 43.48 mV/V
SS74.89 → 69.10 mV/dec
ION/IOFF3.05 → 19.23 ×104
fT491 → 432 GHz
What it buys. Yes, it works. Going from 2.0 to 0.8 nm cuts DIBL from 67.21 to 43.48 mV/V, improves SS, raises ION from 4.10 to 5.28 mA/µm and cuts IOFF from 134.4 to 27.5 nA/µm.
What it costs. Tunneling and speed. The WKB tunneling estimate grows from 0.008% at 2.0 nm to 2.34% at 0.8 nm, about 280 times higher, and fT falls from 491 to 432 GHz.

↓ Thin oxide gives the best control but is tunneling-unsafe, so the 0.8 nm device stays only as a reference. The question becomes: can that control be had without a thin oxide on both sides?

Stage 2

Thick oxide at the source, thin oxide at the drain

The question. The drain is where the damage starts. Can a thin oxide on the drain side alone do most of the job, while the source side stays thick and safe?

What was done. A step oxide (SO-DG) with two sweeps to separate the effects: source oxide fixed at 2.0 nm with drain oxide from 0.8 to 1.6 nm, then drain oxide fixed at 0.8 nm with source oxide from 1.2 to 2.0 nm.

What the plots show. The drain-side oxide has the strong effect: with the source fixed at 2.0 nm, thinning the drain oxide from 1.6 to 0.8 nm improves DIBL from 62.96 to 55.10 mV/V. The source side shows the trade-off: raising it from 1.2 to 2.0 nm adds 7.43 mV/V of DIBL and cuts ION/IOFF by about 42%, but going from 1.6 to 2.0 nm cuts the tunneling estimate by about 43 times. So 2.0 nm source / 0.8 nm drain is selected. The plots show the drain-induced potential change staying more confined to the drain side.

DIBL67.21 → 55.10 mV/V
SS74.89 → 71.22 mV/dec
ION4.10 → 4.34 mA/µm
fT491 → 478 GHz
What it buys. Against the 2.0 nm uniform device, DIBL falls 18% to 55.10 mV/V, SS improves to 71.22 mV/dec, ION rises slightly, IOFF falls from 134.4 to 52.9 nA/µm and ION/IOFF goes from 3.05 to 8.19×104.
What it costs. It is less effective than the 0.8 nm uniform oxide (DIBL 55.10 against 43.48 mV/V), and fT drops about 13 GHz, though it stays at about 97% of the uniform value. The thick source oxide is what keeps tunneling low.

↓ Asymmetric oxide improves the electrostatics without a thin oxide on both sides. The remaining DIBL is addressed next with a dual-material gate.

Stage 3

Raise the source barrier with a dual-material gate

The question. Can a gate made of two materials, with a higher work function over the source side, create a potential step that reduces the drain's grip on the source barrier?

What was done. The selected SO-DG geometry (2.0 nm / 0.8 nm, no further oxide thinning) with the gate split 1:1 into a high-work-function source half and a low-work-function drain half (DMSO-DG). The difference ΔΦ is swept from 0.1 to 0.3 eV.

What the plots show. The boundary between the two gate materials produces a step in the channel potential, and the source-side barrier rises as ΔΦ increases, which matches the fall in IOFF. A smaller step remains visible in the ON state. The fT peak moves to higher VGS as ΔΦ grows, following the rise in Vth.

DIBL55.10 → 32.95 mV/V
IOFF52.9 → 37.5 nA/µm
ION4.34 → 3.34 mA/µm
SS71.22 → 77.82 mV/dec
What it buys. This is the biggest single DIBL cut. At ΔΦ = 0.3 eV DIBL falls about 40% to 32.95 mV/V, lower even than the 0.8 nm uniform oxide, and IOFF falls to 37.54 nA/µm. ΔΦ = 0.3 eV has the lowest DIBL and IOFF of the three and is carried forward.
What it costs. A real one. ION falls from 4.34 to 3.34 mA/µm (17% across the ΔΦ sweep alone), SS worsens to 77.82 mV/dec and Vth rises from 0.358 to 0.463 V. The gain also tapers: DIBL goes 40.57, 34.81, 32.95 mV/V for ΔΦ of 0.1, 0.2, 0.3 eV.

↓ DIBL is much lower, but a lightly doped channel (8×1014 cm−3) still lets the drain depletion region reach toward the source barrier as VDS rises. That is what the halo targets.

Stage 4

Confine the depletion region with halo doping

The question. Can a heavily doped pocket next to each junction keep the drain depletion region from reaching into the channel?

What was done. A 4 nm uniform P+ halo at each source/drain junction, through the full body thickness, on the ΔΦ = 0.3 eV device (Halo-DMSO-DG). Three concentrations: 2×1018, 6×1018 and 1×1019 cm−3, compared with the no-halo device.

What the plots show. Higher local doping narrows the depletion width, roughly as N−1/2, so the drain-side depletion boundary stays close to the junction. That also means diminishing returns: DIBL drops 4.58 mV/V from no halo to 2×1018, but only 1.60 mV/V more going to 1×1019.

DIBL32.95 → 26.77 mV/V
IOFF37.54 → 14.94 nA/µm
ION/IOFF8.91 → 18.05 ×104
ION3.34 → 2.70 mA/µm
What it buys. The halo is particularly good at suppressing the drain-induced rise in OFF-state current: IOFF falls 60.2% and DIBL falls to 26.77 mV/V at 1×1019 cm−3. Because IOFF falls faster than ION, ION/IOFF roughly doubles.
What it costs. ION falls 19.3% at the highest concentration. Vth stays at 0.463 V at 2×1018 but rises to 0.490 V at 1×1019, and fT goes from 475.2 to 476.7 GHz at the lowest level and down to 466.1 GHz at the highest. SS barely moves (77.08–77.27 mV/dec).

↓ 2×1018 cm−3 is the best trade-off: IOFF 20.19 nA/µm, DIBL 28.37 mV/V, Vth unchanged and fT slightly higher. The halo is another design trade-off, not a free improvement.

Results

  • DIBL cut by 60% versus the 2.0 nm uniform-oxide baseline: 67.21 → 55.10 → 32.95 → 26.77 mV/V across the step-oxide, DMG and halo stages.
  • ION/IOFF improved nearly sixfold, from 3.05×104 to 18.05×104, with IOFF falling from 134.4 to 14.94 nA/µm.
  • The gains cost something. ION falls from 4.10 to 2.70 mA/µm, SS ends at 77.27 mV/dec (best was 71.22 at the step-oxide stage), Vth rises to 0.490 V and fT drops from 491 to 466 GHz at the highest halo level.
  • Best halo trade-off at 2×1018 cm−3: DIBL 28.37 mV/V, IOFF 20.19 nA/µm, Vth unchanged and fT slightly higher. Beyond that the returns shrink while penalties grow.

Putting it together

DIBL falls 67.21 → 55.10 → 32.95 → 26.77 mV/V across the stages, 60% below the 2.0 nm baseline, while SS never improves beyond the Stage 2 value of 71.22 mV/dec. The three techniques act as complementary controls with different penalties. The step oxide is the cheap one and even raises ION. The dual-material gate gives the largest DIBL cut but costs ION, Vth and SS. The halo does the most for leakage but costs ION, and at high concentration Vth and fT as well.

How it compares

The step-oxide gain matches the direction reported by Al-Jawadi et al. for a source-thick, drain-thin 20 nm MOSFET, which improved leakage and drive current (they did not report DIBL). The DMG trade-off of lower DIBL with higher SS and lower ION is consistent with work on DMG junctionless FinFETs. The halo gain is smaller than the near-zero DIBL reported for a 14 nm p+-pocket DG MOSFET, but the structures and halo profiles differ. Unlike earlier studies that use these techniques alone or in pairs, this one applies them in sequence on the same planar 20 nm structure.

Limits

  • The DMG segment ratio is fixed at 1:1; other ratios could shift the trade-off.
  • The halo is a uniform box profile; non-uniform profiles have been reported to give different trade-offs.
  • The drift-diffusion model has no tunneling, so IOFF is channel leakage only and gate tunneling appears only through the WKB estimate.
